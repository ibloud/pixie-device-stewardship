# The system should remember

I did not set out to design a file-naming protocol. I was trying to make something with another artist.

The collaboration exposed a familiar failure: in the middle of creative work, the person was expected to stop, decide what a file should be called, remember where it belonged, distinguish it from earlier versions, and communicate that private filing logic to somebody else.

We often describe the resulting disorder as a discipline problem. The artist should have named the file better. The user should have organized the folder. The person should have remembered where they put it.

That explanation protects the software from criticism.

The device already knows the date, application, file type, recent project, and often the people involved. It can preserve version history. It can index content. Increasingly, it can understand ordinary language. Yet it still asks the human to translate a creative moment into machine housekeeping.

For people whose attention, memory, energy, or executive function varies, that translation is not a harmless extra step. It can break concentration, make returning to work harder, and create friction between collaborators who are each trying to reconstruct a system that should have documented itself.

## The PIXIE hypothesis

PIXIE begins with a simple proposition:

> The person should describe their intent. The system should handle its machinery.

An artist should be able to say:

> “This is the bridge idea for Fifty Ways. Charlie is on it. I want to remember that it came after we changed the rhythm.”

The system can derive a stable filename, version, project location, collaborator tag, and provenance entry. Later, the artist should be able to ask, “Where is that bridge idea Charlie was on?” without recalling the generated name.

This is not permission for a device to reorganize a person's life secretly. PIXIE is preview-first and reversible. It must show what it understood, admit uncertainty, ask before moving anything, and make its learned rules visible and correctable.

## Neurodivergence is not a defect report

PIXIE is informed by ADHD and cognitive-access experience, but its purpose is not to make neurodivergent people behave more like an operating system expects.

The design question is the reverse: why has the operating system not learned to meet the person?

W3C cognitive-accessibility guidance already encourages clear steps, reduced input demands, personalization, and support for people returning after distraction. PIXIE applies that direction to the operating system's file, search, versioning, and recovery layers.

## Why begin with creators

Creative production makes the failure visible. A session can generate recordings, stems, exports, edits, references, screenshots, drafts, and finals across several applications and collaborators. The work arrives faster than anyone can maintain a perfect taxonomy.

Musicians, filmmakers, designers, photographers, writers, and game developers are not edge cases. They are a demanding test of whether a device can preserve human intention without interrupting it.

## What we are asking Apple—and every platform provider—to change

We want operating systems to provide a user-owned Device Steward layer that:

- accepts natural-language project context from creative applications;
- generates names, versions, metadata, and suggested destinations;
- maintains a portable provenance manifest;
- retrieves files through human descriptions and relationships;
- preserves the person's place when work is interrupted;
- previews every organizational action;
- makes every change reversible; and
- exposes learned preferences for inspection, correction, export, and deletion.

We will begin by documenting consent-cleared creative workflow barriers, testing a read-only prototype, and submitting reproducible findings through official accessibility and product-feedback channels.

## A campaign, not a diagnosis

PIXIE does not know what somebody feels because they repeat a step or abandon a task. It can notice that the interface is not helping and offer a different path. Observation is not diagnosis. Assistance is not surveillance.

The people who reveal a design failure are not automatically the subjects of a public story. Our case records separate direct evidence, personal accounts, interpretation, and untested hypotheses. Identifying details require explicit permission.

## Help us make the burden visible

If a creative tool has ever made you stop creating to serve its filing system, document the task—not your diagnosis.

What were you trying to make? What did the device ask you to remember? What did it already know? What would a respectful recovery path have looked like?

That is the evidence operating-system teams need. And it is the beginning of a system that speaks human.
