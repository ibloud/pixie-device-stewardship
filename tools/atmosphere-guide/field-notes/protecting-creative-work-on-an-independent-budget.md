# Play It Forward: Protecting Creative Work on an Independent Budget

**November 24, 2026**  
*Fair compensation, brand lore and the cost of getting the right legal help.*

A musician can develop a world through songs, clothes, recurring characters and art. A name or symbol that begins in a story may later appear on a product label. When that happens, the creator needs to know who made the artwork, who controls its use, and which questions warrant a lawyer’s review before money goes into a release.

## Pay people for the work they do

Mike Monteiro’s CreativeMornings talk [*Fuck You, Pay Me*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jVkLVRt6c1U) grounds this discussion in getting paid for creative work and seeking counsel when the stakes merit it. For an independent studio, a lawyer who understands the work can help define the actual engagement, identify what needs judgment, and explain which deliverables the fee covers. A creator can prepare the records in advance and ask for a quote tied to a specific question.

A production template can help capture an agreed deal. [Thoolie](https://thoolie.com/) offers lawyer-drafted film templates and describes limits on individualized advice. Preserve signed and dated agreements and verify that the contract covers the intended medium and use. [Thanks Mom](https://www.thanksmom.ai/faq) describes a separate service focused on U.S. trademark clearance. A search, a filing, an ownership agreement and defense of a dispute each have their own scope and cost.

## What the courts decided


- *Jack Daniel’s Properties v. VIP Products* (U.S. Supreme Court, 2023). When the challenged use functions as branding for the seller’s own goods, the ordinary trademark confusion inquiry applies. Parody can still matter in that inquiry. Review how a symbol appears on packaging, merchandise and store pages. [Supreme Court opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/22-148_3e04.pdf).
- *DC Comics v. Towle* (Ninth Circuit, 2015). The court recognized copyright in the Batmobile as a sufficiently distinctive character on the record before it. Compare specific design features and document authorship and permissions before drawing conclusions from a visual resemblance. [Appellate opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/13-55484/13-55484-2015-09-23.html).

For commissioned artwork, record the exact asset and use, then verify the signed license or assignment. U.S. copyright transfers generally require a signed writing. Commissioned work qualifies as work made for hire only in limited circumstances. [17 U.S.C. §§ 201 and 204](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap2.html) · [Copyright Office work-made-for-hire guidance](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf).
## Where public policy could help

The Copyright Claims Board handles eligible copyright disputes up to $30,000 and respondents can opt out. Trademark clearance and trademark disputes fall outside that route. A practical policy question is whether early, affordable advice and clearer access to dispute information help creators preserve their rights. [Copyright Claims Board](https://ccb.gov/access-a-claim/) · [Loptr Lab’s policy research and public review](https://ibloud.github.io/legal-rights/#creator-rights-policy).

## What would count as evidence of savings?

Compare equivalent project briefs prepared with and without a structured creator-side record. Track preparation time, legal review cost when it occurs, omissions spotted in review, and questions that still required professional judgment. Include rework and accessibility barriers in the total. A shorter intake that increases missed ownership or permission questions fails the test. Any savings remain a hypothesis.

For a budget reference, the [USPTO base application fee](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/basics/how-much-does-it-cost) is $350 per class, with additional fees possible. Its [Minnesota resource page](https://www.uspto.gov/about-us/uspto-office-locations/minnesota) lists trademark clinics, subject to intake and eligibility. The [USPTO likelihood-of-confusion guidance](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/search/likelihood-confusion) explains why the mark and the related goods and services both matter.
