# What Are We Asking People to Pay For?

Field Note 014 · November 23, 2026

Suppose a musician asks us to help archive a release. By the end of the afternoon there is a page with a recording credit, a link to the music, and a contact for permissions. It looks finished. The real test comes a month later, when the musician has another release. Who has the password? Who knows how to change the credit? Whose invoice arrives if they want to move?

That is why Loptr Lab is building the archive around its own studio work first. We need to find out how long an install takes, what breaks during a restore, and whether someone else can use the instructions. In the proposed Mayhem session, a second creator gets the notes and tries the handoff. A missing step belongs in the guide. Nobody owes us free troubleshooting to earn a place in the room.

There is work here worth charging for. A client might commission an accessible release template, ask us to configure a personal data server, or pay for a lesson using their own catalog. Maintenance has a different price because updates, monitoring and recovery take time after installation. We want the quote to name that work and the handoff to leave the client in control.

Our first public cost record should count preparation, teaching, follow-up and hosting alongside revenue. It should also answer one question a sales total cannot: what could the participant do on their own afterward? If they teach the next creator, that belongs in the account of what the studio produced.
