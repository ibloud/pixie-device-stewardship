The Reality of Change

Dominique Devereaux, MPA, MBA · Founder & Lead Systems Architect, Loptr Lab · PhD student, Organizational Leadership – Information Technology (author-reported). Google Scholar · Author credentials and CV

When institutions change their language, but inequality survives another generation

Change should be measured by what people inherit, not simply by what institutions announce.

Who gains access to education? Who becomes a decision-maker? Who owns the technology? Who controls the resources? Who can challenge a system without losing access to the services upon which they depend?

These questions are central to Loptr Lab's investigation into organizational leadership and the design of humane technology.

The inheritance of institutional decisions

The Minnesota Family Investment Program (MFIP) provides a historical case. Between 1994 and 1996, more than 14,000 families were randomly assigned to groups in a welfare-policy evaluation. Researchers subsequently examined economic outcomes and the experiences of parents and children. Institutional decisions can have consequences extending beyond the immediate participant.

Our founder, a former participant, has sought historical records concerning research oversight, participant protections, consent, data governance and institutional accountability. Requests initiated in September 2026 resulted in agency referrals, archival directions and the closure of a state records request. Recovered correspondence does not establish where all requested records are held or what protections governed every aspect of the research.

An unanswered question is not proof of misconduct. But it remains an unanswered question.

What has changed for the next generation?

National science and engineering doctorate data indicates that Black representation among U.S. citizen and permanent resident recipients increased from approximately 5% in 2004 to 6% in 2024. More people earned doctorates, but proportional representation changed relatively little.

Historically Black colleges and universities continue to make a substantial contribution to developing Black doctoral talent. Their contribution deserves recognition as evidence that institutional environments and educational pathways matter. We must also examine what happens beyond graduation: who receives funding, enters leadership, influences policy and has authority to design systems governing other people's lives. Doctorate attainment is not a direct measure of leadership representation or completion rates.

A diversity statement cannot answer those questions. Outcomes can.

Why we are building on the Atmosphere

Our interest in the AT Protocol is not simply about finding another social network. It is about reducing dependence on centralized systems that determine which voices are discoverable, which conversations receive attention and how much control people retain over their digital identities and published work.

Open protocols do not eliminate censorship, racial bias, moderation or algorithmic filtering. They can provide alternatives to dependence on a single platform and its distribution decisions. We want our research, questions, corrections and creative work to remain available through independently maintained infrastructure.

Visibility should not be confused with authority. Popularity should not be confused with truth.

Designing humanity into technology

Through Loptr Lab, PIXIE and Duet, we are examining how rules, incentives, accessibility, governance and technology influence human agency.

We test the system, not the worth of the person using it.

Duet provides a bounded environment for discussing fairness, competition, cooperation, accessibility and recovery. It does not diagnose players or establish their value as people. Our wider research asks whether the systems we build today can interrupt inherited inequities rather than reproduce them.

What would constitute meaningful change?

  • Greater access to education and leadership.
  • Measurable reductions in racial and disability disparities.
  • Transparent institutional decisions and accessible records.
  • Meaningful consent and control over personal information.
  • Sustainable opportunities rather than permanent dependency.
  • Technology that respects people regardless of their productivity, income or social influence.

We welcome evidence, criticism and informed discussion from educators, HBCU scholars, researchers, independent developers, accessibility specialists and people with lived experience of institutional systems.

We are not asking anyone to accept our conclusions without scrutiny. We are asking that the same scrutiny be applied to the institutions claiming progress.

The reality of change is what the next generation no longer has to endure.
