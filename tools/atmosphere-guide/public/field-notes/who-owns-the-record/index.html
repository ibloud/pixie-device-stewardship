Who Owns the Record?

Dominique Devereaux, MPA, MBA · Founder & Lead Systems Architect, Loptr Lab · PhD student, Organizational Leadership – Information Technology (author-reported). Google Scholar · Author credentials and CV

Research participants, data privacy and the institutional right to remember

Institutions collect information about people to administer services, measure outcomes, conduct research and develop public policy. What happens when those people later want to understand how their information was used?

Revisiting MFIP

As a former participant in the Minnesota Family Investment Program, our founder has sought historical records concerning the program's research, administration and participant protections.

The inquiries initiated on September 21, 2026, requested information about research oversight, consent, random assignment, data sharing, retention and records disposition. According to the previously summarized correspondence, on September 24 Minnesota's Department of Children, Youth, and Families closed a request, stating that the requested historical records were no longer held by the relevant unit and directing attention toward state archives.

The recovered summary did not identify an archival accession number, a specific disposition date or a complete description of the search undertaken. This does not prove unlawful destruction or unauthorized research. It does leave questions about how historical research accountability can be established.

The asymmetry of information

Public institutions may hold substantial information about individuals while those individuals have limited knowledge of how the information was classified, shared, retained or interpreted. That imbalance becomes particularly important when administrative information influences access to employment, education, housing or financial assistance.

People should have understandable ways to investigate the decisions that affect their lives, subject to legitimate privacy protections and applicable law.

Privacy is more than confidentiality

  • What information was collected and why?
  • Did research participation require consent or another lawful authorization?
  • Which institutions received the information?
  • How long were records retained?
  • Could incorrect information be challenged?
  • What oversight existed when information was reused?

The absence of answers does not establish a violation. It establishes the need to obtain and examine the relevant evidence.

Designing better systems

Through PIXIE, Loptr Lab is exploring technology that prioritizes consent, transparent permissions, data stewardship and human control. Those principles should apply not only to future technology but also to how we evaluate existing institutional systems.

People should not become permanent data subjects while losing the practical ability to understand their own institutional history.

Public reference: MDRC's published MFIP evaluation.
