Your Infrastructure Is a Business Decision

Independence, network effects, and the economics of creative ownership.

There is a difference between participating in a network and surrendering ownership of your place within it.

Artists, independent creators, entrepreneurs, and small communities are routinely encouraged to join platforms, sign agreements, surrender percentages, and outsource the difficult parts of their businesses. Sometimes that is the correct decision. Infrastructure costs money. Distribution takes work. Expertise has value. Collaboration can create opportunities that would be impossible to achieve alone.

But there is a question that too often goes unanswered: What are you actually paying for, and what are you giving away in exchange?

The price of convenience

A platform can offer immediate access to an audience. A label can provide financing, marketing, distribution, and professional relationships. A management company can help navigate opportunities. A hosting provider can make technical infrastructure disappear into the background.

These services can be valuable. The problem isn't paying for them. The problem is not knowing what the value is, what the cost will become over time, or whether the dependency is permanent.

A percentage of revenue that seems insignificant today can become a substantial expense when the work succeeds. An agreement that seems reasonable when you have no audience may become extraordinarily expensive when you have built one. Evaluate an opportunity across its lifecycle—not simply by what it offers on the day you sign.

The artist is often the least educated participant in the transaction

We teach people how to produce creative work, improve their craft, build an audience, generate attention, and become commercially attractive. But we rarely teach them to calculate the value of their intellectual property, understand ownership structures, negotiate licensing agreements, or determine the economic consequences of surrendering control.

This creates a structural imbalance. The artist arrives with the work. The intermediary arrives with the vocabulary, contracts, financial models, and experience of negotiating hundreds of similar transactions. The imbalance doesn't necessarily mean the intermediary is acting in bad faith. It means the artist is negotiating without equivalent information.

Information is leverage.

Resistance can look like dependency

In The War of Art, Steven Pressfield describes Resistance as the force that prevents us from doing the work we are meant to do. Resistance can manifest as procrastination, fear, self-doubt, and the endless search for permission.

But Resistance can also disguise itself as professional advice: You aren't a businessperson. You don't understand technology. You need a gatekeeper. You need a platform. You need someone else to manage your career. You need to surrender control to gain access.

There are circumstances where professional assistance is genuinely necessary. But there is a difference between hiring expertise and transferring your agency.

Learning economics doesn't mean becoming an accountant. Learning technology doesn't mean becoming a systems administrator. Understanding a contract doesn't mean refusing legal counsel. The objective isn't to do everything yourself. It's to understand enough to make an informed decision about who does what, why, at what price, and under whose authority.

Networks create value. Ownership creates leverage.

A network is valuable because other people are there. A distribution platform is valuable because it makes discovery easier. A community is valuable because relationships create opportunities. But the network's value and your ownership of the network are not the same thing.

The AT Protocol offers an example of separating participation from infrastructure ownership. People can participate in the Atmosphere without operating their own Personal Data Server (PDS). Those who choose to run their own PDS can take greater responsibility for their identity infrastructure. The protocol supports portable identity and repositories, but migration is still a process with technical and operational considerations.

You can participate without owning infrastructure. You can own infrastructure without isolating yourself from the network. You can build relationships without making a single provider the permanent custodian of your identity.

That is a different model from the platform bargain in which your audience, content, and business relationships can become inseparable from the platform that distributes them.

Independence has a cost

Running your own infrastructure is not free. You have to understand security, backups, maintenance, updates, monitoring, and recovery. A self-hosted system that is poorly maintained may be less reliable than a professionally managed service.

Independence without competence can become another form of vulnerability. The answer isn't to romanticize self-hosting. The answer is to calculate the cost of ownership and compare it with the cost of dependency.

Sometimes the right decision is to pay a provider. Sometimes the right decision is to build your own infrastructure. Sometimes the right decision is to share infrastructure with a community whose interests are aligned with yours. What matters is that the decision is yours—and that you understand the consequences.

The economics of creative freedom

The greatest value of independence may not be the money saved. It may be the decisions you are no longer required to make according to someone else's incentives.

When you own your infrastructure, you can decide what to build. When you understand your economics, you can decide what to accept. When you control your identity, you can decide where to participate. When you own your intellectual property, you can decide how it is licensed. When you have alternatives, you can negotiate. When you understand the cost of dependency, you can distinguish a partnership from a transaction that simply benefits the other party.

None of this guarantees success. But it changes the terms under which success becomes possible.

Learn the economics before you negotiate your independence away

The purpose of independence isn't to reject every partnership, platform, service, or intermediary. It's to build the capacity to evaluate them.

Convenience has value. Distribution has value. Expertise has value. But so does ownership. So does control. So does the ability to leave.

The next time someone offers to solve your business problem by taking a percentage of everything you create, ask what the service is worth, what alternatives exist, what the agreement costs over time, and whether you could build some of that capability yourself.

Learn the economics. Build your leverage. Own your exit.
