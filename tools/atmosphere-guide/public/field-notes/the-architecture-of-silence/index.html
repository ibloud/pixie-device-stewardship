The Architecture of Silence

Dominique Devereaux · Founder & Lead Systems Architect, Loptr Lab · PhD student, Organizational Leadership – Information Technology (author-reported). Google Scholar · Author profile

When institutional accountability becomes a forwarding address

How many institutions must a person contact before someone accepts responsibility for investigating a problem?

Since living in Minneapolis, our founder has raised concerns involving discrimination, accessibility, administrative practices, personal records and institutional accountability.

The correspondence tells a story that is more complicated than simple silence. There are acknowledgments, referrals, requests to repeat information, closed records requests and directions to contact another authority. What remains missing in several cases is a substantive determination.

The administrative journey

In September 2026, inquiries concerning historical Minnesota Family Investment Program research were submitted to state, county, university and federal institutions. One state agency closed its request and referred the matter elsewhere. Another acknowledged a search before closing the request because the records were reportedly no longer held.

The recovered correspondence does not identify a complete chain of custody for the requested historical records. Other inquiries concerning accessibility, housing compliance and institutional grievance procedures followed similarly fragmented paths. Some administrative matters progressed. Others remain unresolved in the correspondence available for review.

An institution should receive credit for what it actually resolves. Equally, an acknowledgment should not be counted as resolution when the underlying question remains unanswered.

The cost of institutional fragmentation

Every referral transfers work back to the person seeking assistance. That person must understand jurisdiction, locate the appropriate authority, preserve correspondence, repeat information, navigate intake requirements and determine whether another deadline applies.

For people experiencing disability, economic insecurity or other structural disadvantages, this work can become an additional barrier to accessing rights and services. The burden of navigating an institution is itself a design issue.

What are institutions measuring?

An organization may record a request as closed. But was the question answered? A complaint may have been forwarded. But was the alleged conduct investigated? An accommodation may have been acknowledged. But was the person able to participate effectively?

These distinctions matter when institutions report their performance and claim progress toward equity.

What accountability should look like

At minimum, people should be able to obtain:

  • An identifiable authority responsible for their inquiry.
  • A clear explanation of jurisdiction and applicable procedures.
  • An accessible method of communication.
  • A documented determination or explanation of why one cannot be made.
  • An understandable path to review, appeal or correction.

These are proposed standards for humane institutional design, not assertions that every organization has identical legal obligations.

Leaving a record

We are publishing through independently maintained infrastructure because these questions should remain discoverable beyond the attention cycle of a social media platform. The public record should distinguish what happened, what was alleged, what was answered and what remains unknown.

Silence is not always the absence of communication. Sometimes it is the absence of accountability despite an abundance of communication.

Evidence boundary: This article draws on previously summarized historical correspondence and first-person accounts. It does not establish unlawful discrimination, intentional obstruction or a confirmed privacy violation. Private correspondence and identifying participant records are withheld. The underlying correspondence was not independently re-reviewed for this publication.
